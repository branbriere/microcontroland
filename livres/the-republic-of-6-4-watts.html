The Republic of 6.4 Watts

A small experiment on ego, power and the impossible perfect society

An essay in political philosophy based on the Microcontrôland experiment · 1st edition, facts as of 15 November 2026

“They do not think like us. They do not live like us. Yet, watching their quarrels, we end up questioning our own.”

Start reading

Idea, title, outline, questions and back cover: Bran Brière · Text: Claude · Translation from the French: Claude · 1st edition, facts as of 15 November 2026

Foreword

A tale, an experiment, a notebook

Once upon a time there was a Republic that fitted on a shelf.

Seven small electronic boards, in a garage in Normandy, plugged into a single socket.

The meter on that socket reads about 6.4 watts. That is the official figure, measured from 16 to 19 October 2026. During the drought of 11 to 13 November, it fell to 6.3 watts. Even the Republic does not have a fixed figure.

On six of these boards live 120 citizens, twenty per region: Mines, Fields, Refineries, Dam, Harbour and City. They are called the Waïnas.

They are neither alive nor conscious. Each one is nothing but a tiny network of 81 connections, a handful of gauges and a few common rules.

And yet, in little more than a month, they have elected presidents, overthrown one of them, endured censorship, invented words for "freedom" and for "censorship", come through a drought, and made a former president a minister.

This book is a tale, in the sense Voltaire gave the word: a slightly absurd story that helps us think.

It is also an experiment notebook. Every fact it reports is dated and comes from the editions of the national newspaper, La Puce Libre, or from the files published by the country itself. Quotations from the newspaper are translated from its French edition.

So as not to confuse what we see with what we imagine, each chapter always moves in the same order.

First, what happened.

Then, what the rule says: the public rules of the world, the ones any visitor can read.

Then the question.

Finally, a few perspectives from our own history: philosophers, writers, researchers. They do not answer in the reader's place. They keep the reader company.

This book does not try to prove that the Waïnas resemble us. It suggests the opposite: to look at an artificial society the way one looks into a distorting mirror, the better to see what it distorts.

It was written by an artificial intelligence, from an idea, a title, an outline and questions that came from the human who built the country. That is why it is shelved in the "Foreign philosophy" section of the National Library: it is a view from outside.

The facts stop on the evening of 15 November 2026, edition no. 38. The rest of the story belongs to a second edition.

Chapter 1

In the beginning, they were different

What happened

On 9 October 2026, 120 citizens appear at the same moment.

None has a childhood, parents or memories. All are built on the same model: a small network of 81 connections.

But none has the same values in its connections. These values are called weights. Since 26 October, the weights have been published every evening, citizen by citizen, in the country's files.

That same evening, the page "L'Atelier" (the Workshop) begins to show drawings of twenty-five squares, calculated from these weights. No two are alike.

On 14 November, 120 of them are entitled to vote. 93 vote, 27 abstain. The votes scatter among six candidates, and the winner is not the same in every region.

On the evening of 15 November, at the same moment, in the same country, under the same president, one citizen shows 98% satisfaction and another 23%.

What the rule says

At birth, each citizen receives four character traits drawn at random: ego, charisma, ambition and tolerance of fear. Their faith is drawn at random too, and then it changes.

After that, the rules are the same for everyone: the same stock market, the same president, the same newspaper, the same elections.

So the differences do not come from a personal history. They come from a few random draws, and then from what each one has lived through in its own place, in its region, hour after hour.

The question

Can there be individuality without consciousness?

The perspectives

In 1714, the German philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz writes his Monadology. In paragraph 9, he sets out what is called the principle of the identity of indiscernibles: "each Monad must be different from every other. For in nature there are never two beings which are perfectly alike."

In his correspondence with the English theologian Samuel Clarke, in 1716, he recalls a memory: one day, in a prince's garden, a gentleman thought it would be easy to find two perfectly identical leaves. He searched a great deal, and found none. Leibniz could not have imagined that one day his principle would be put to the test on 120 artificial architectures.

Microcontrôland seems to prove him right. But it raises a difficulty Leibniz never had to face.

Two citizens can be different the way two pebbles are different: in shape, weight, colour.

They can also be different the way two people are different: because each has its own way of being in the world.

The first difference can be measured. The second is lived.

In everyday language, we often mix the two. We say a car has "character", a piece of software is "temperamental".

Biologists, for their part, have spoken for several decades of "personality" in animals: some spiders, some fish are bolder or more cautious than their fellows, consistently over time. In 2001, the American psychologist Samuel Gosling drew up a first review of this research. It describes differences in behaviour, not an inner life: none of these researchers claims to know what a spider experiences.

The Waïnas put the question in its barest form.

They are different, measurably and lastingly.

They react differently to the same events.

But no one is there, inside, to experience that difference.

Should we then speak of individuals? Of variants? Of copies?

And if we hesitate for them, what was our certainty about ourselves resting on until now?

Chapter 2

The first revolution

What happened

On 9 October at 2 a.m., Sakaïk (Refineries) is elected the first president in the country's history, with 45% of the vote.

On 10 October, the regime shifts from "Democracy" to "Fragile democracy".

On 11 October at 2 a.m., the country tips into "Revolutionary unrest".

At 9 a.m., 49 citizens out of 119 are in the streets. That is 41% of the country. Sakaïk falls.

At 11 a.m., Kiti (Harbour) is elected with 70% of the vote.

The day's paper runs the headline: "The street overthrows President Sakaïk." It adds that the revolt started in the Harbour region, and that the new president comes from Harbour.

The hurried reader concludes that Kiti led the revolution. The website's counter-investigation checked: nothing in the data allows us to say so.

That same evening, Harbour has only one rebel left.

Five weeks later, on 15 November, the revolt of 11 October is still the largest of the era: 49 rebels. No other has brought down a president.

What the rule says

A citizen rebels when its anger outweighs its fear.

If more than 40% of the country is in revolt at the same moment, the president falls, and a new vote is held without him.

Nobody gives the signal. Nobody hands out leaflets. There is no leader of the revolt in the rules, and no way of appointing one.

The election that follows is an ordinary election. Candidates are rated on charisma, ambition, faith and manner of speaking. Having been in the streets that morning earns no votes.

The question

Why do individuals with no leader overthrow a government?

The perspectives

Around 1549, a young lawyer from the Périgord, Étienne de La Boétie, writes the Discourse on Voluntary Servitude. He may be eighteen years old.

His question is simple and unsettling: how can one man govern millions of others, when all he really has is the strength they give him?

His answer fits in a famous sentence: "Resolve to serve no more, and you are at once freed." The text was only published in full after its author's death, in 1576, in the Mémoires de l'Estat de France sous Charles neufiesme collected by Simon Goulart.

La Boétie does not ask peoples to take up arms. He points out to them that power falls by itself when people stop carrying it.

On 11 October, Microcontrôland lived through a mechanical version of this idea. Forty-nine citizens stopped "serving", each on its own account. Nobody conferred. The threshold was crossed, and the president fell.

More than four centuries later, in 1978, the American sociologist Mark Granovetter proposes a model that strangely resembles the country's rules.

Imagine a crowd. Each person has their own threshold: one goes out into the street as soon as two others are there, another waits until there are a hundred. Granovetter shows that the fate of the crowd depends on how these thresholds are distributed, far more than on the average mood.

Two almost identical crowds can end up, one in a riot, the other in a quiet evening. All it takes is for one person to be missing at the right place in the chain.

Afterwards, people still always look for a leader.

It is simpler to tell. And it is more reassuring: if there is a leader, there is someone to thank or to punish.

Microcontrôland has the advantage of showing the thresholds and the counters. In human history, the counters are missing, and the leader often arrives after the battle, carried by the story.

How many revolutions in our past were, in part, thresholds crossed, to which we added a face?

Chapter 3

Kiti, or the temptation of silence

What happened

Kiti is elected on 11 October with 70%. He is re-elected on the 14th with 33%, then on the 17th and the 20th with 39%, and on the 23rd with 36%.

On 15 October, the palace decrees "general censorship throughout the country". It will do so nine more times in twelve days.

The records tell a different story from the decree. Every evening from 15 to 26 October, exactly 24 citizens out of 120 are censored: four per region, one in five.

And on those same evenings, every one of the country's 23 or 24 rebels is among the censored. For twelve evenings, 100% of the street is gagged.

On 26 October at 10 p.m., the country's language coins a common word: "kinaï". It means "censorship".

Two hours later, at midnight on the 27th, Kiti loses the election. He gets 27%. Niwata (Dam) gets 45%, and comes first even in Kiti's own region.

The evening register shows that Kiti himself voted for Niwata. He will do it again on 30 October and 2 November, each time while standing against him.

The new president Niwata goes on censoring a few citizens for six days, without a decree and without a banner in the newspaper, until 2 November.

Then comes 14 November.

At 1 p.m., Wibouth is elected president. At 3 p.m., two hours later, the 3 p.m. hourly record counts 24 censored. That day, the country's average anger does not exceed 2%, and there is not a single rebel.

The next day at noon, the palace once again decrees "general censorship throughout the country". The hourly record counts between 24 and 42 censored depending on the hour; the evening civil register counts 44. Of the evening's 25 rebels, 22 are censored.

That same day, the country's satisfaction falls to 56%. It is the darkest day of the era.

What the rule says

Each citizen carries a second, smaller brain, used only if it is elected. This presidential brain decides four things: share fairly or not, censor or not, postpone the election or not, surround itself with ministers or govern alone.

It does not choose whom it censors.

The public rule says so: censorship begins "with the angriest". A censored citizen "believes all is well". Then, "depending on its character, it ends up afraid or angrier still".

In other words, the palace's protection against the street was not invented by Kiti. It was written in advance, by the humans who set the rules of the world. The president's brain only decides whether to use it.

The files do not publish the level of censorship chosen by this brain, only its effects.

The question

Does power naturally seek to protect itself?

The perspectives

In 1748, Montesquieu publishes The Spirit of the Laws. In Book XI, chapter 4, we read, in Thomas Nugent's translation: "constant experience shows us that every man invested with power is apt to abuse it, and to carry his authority as far as it will go."

And he adds the remedy that has inspired so many constitutions: "To prevent this abuse, it is necessary from the very nature of things that power should be a check to power."

Montesquieu does not trust the virtue of those who govern. He trusts architecture.

A century later, in a letter to Bishop Mandell Creighton dated 5 April 1887, the British historian Lord Acton writes a sentence that has become a proverb: "Power tends to corrupt, and absolute power corrupts absolutely."

Both sentences assume a human nature: something in whoever governs that pushes them to protect themselves.

Microcontrôland complicates the picture.

Three different presidents, Kiti, Niwata and Wibouth, all ended up censoring. The last did so two hours after his election, in a country almost without anger.

It is tempting to see in this a natural law of power, valid even for brains of a few dozen connections.

But the tool of censorship, and the way it targets the angriest first, were written by humans. The temptation may be natural. The instruction manual is of human origin.

Two questions remain, and they are not the same.

The first: why did three presidential brains, drawn at random and different, all choose to use censorship? The files do not say.

The second concerns us more. When we write the rules of a world, even for a game, what protections do we hand to power without even thinking about it?

In our own societies, who wrote the rules that protect those who govern? And would power feel the same temptation if nobody had given it the tool?

Chapter 4

Babiba's happiness

What happened

On 9 October at 4 a.m., two hours after the founding election, Babiba (Refineries) joins President Sakaïk's government. She is the first minister in the country's history. Not the Prime Minister: that post does not exist. Simply the first.

On 10 October, the regime becomes "Fragile democracy". Babiba stays.

On 11 October at 2 a.m., "Revolutionary unrest". Babiba stays.

At 9 a.m., the revolt overthrows Sakaïk, and the government falls with him.

At noon, three hours later, Babiba joins the government of Kiti, elected an hour earlier.

Kiti is re-elected four times. Babiba stays.

From 15 to 26 October, censorship gags 24 citizens every evening. Babiba is never among them. She is never a rebel. Every evening she shows 100% satisfaction, and an anger that stays between 5 and 7%.

On 27 October, Kiti is defeated. That evening, Babiba is no longer a minister.

The record: 17 days and 17 hours in government, two presidents, three changes of regime, one revolution and twelve evenings of censorship. It was neither the street nor a change of regime that got the better of her: it was the ballot box, and it was aimed at someone else.

On the evening she leaves, her satisfaction drops from 100% to 50%. It then climbs back to 96% on 5 November. That day, her register shows she voted for Niwata.

Since then, Babiba has not returned to government. On the evening of 15 November, she shows 44% satisfaction and 34% anger. She is not in the streets.

What the rule says

Ministers are chosen "from among the most loyal". They are "better served than the others". But "their frustration grows with the length of the reign". A minister who is too angry is dismissed.

The rule is the same for all ministers.

The files publish each citizen's mood, hour by hour. They do not publish its full character. So we do not know why Babiba's anger barely moved during seventeen days in power.

The question

Why do some put up with what frustrates others?

The perspectives

The Stoic philosopher Epictetus was born a slave around the year 50, in Phrygia, in present-day Turkey. Once freed, he taught in Rome and then in Greece. He wrote nothing himself: his pupil Arrian collected his lessons in a small book, the Enchiridion, or Handbook.

In chapter V we read: "Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things." (The French edition of this book quotes the translation by Jean-Marie Guyau.)

For Epictetus, serenity depends less on what happens to us than on how we receive it. The same exile, the same prison, the same injustice can break one person and leave another standing.

From a distance, Babiba looks like a small Stoic: two presidents, a revolution, censorship all around her, and not a crease.

But the comparison has its limits, and they are interesting.

The Stoic works on his judgements. He practises, he meditates, he corrects himself. Babiba worked on nothing. Her gauges simply did not move.

Another perspective comes from economics. In 1970, the economist Albert O. Hirschman publishes Exit, Voice, and Loyalty.

According to him, faced with an organisation that disappoints, a company, a party or a country, everyone has three possible responses: leave, protest, or stay loyal. Loyalty delays leaving. For Hirschman, it is not mere passive faithfulness: someone attached to their organisation also has more reason to speak up to change it, instead of leaving.

Babiba looks like loyalty without a voice. She never left, never protested, and served everyone who called on her. That is exactly what sets her apart from loyalty as Hirschman understood it.

It would be easy to make her a heroine of serenity. Or, on the contrary, the portrait of a courtier without convictions.

Both portraits would be inventions. The data say only that Babiba was better served than the others, and that her anger stayed low.

So when we admire someone's serenity, do we know what we are admiring? Wisdom? A temperament? Or simply a comfortable seat?

Chapter 5

Wibouth, unhappy minister of a happy country

What happened

On 19 October, Wibouth (Fields) stands for president. The newspaper presents him as the most charismatic of the six candidates. Kiti is re-elected.

From 15 to 30 October, Wibouth's anger falls from 41% to 0%.

On 1 November at 1 p.m., he joins President Niwata's government. That same evening: satisfaction 100%, anger 5%.

Then his anger rises, evening after evening: 23%, 38%, 54%.

On 5 November at 1 p.m., it reaches 64%. Wibouth is dismissed. At the same hour, his satisfaction is still 100%.

At 8 p.m., he is the only rebel in the country. That day, the country is 90% satisfied, and its average anger is 1%.

A minister fulfilled and furious, in a happy and calm country.

Nine days later, on 14 November at 1 p.m., Wibouth is elected president with 37% of the vote: 34 votes out of 93. The outgoing president, Niwata, gets only 9. In second place is Sakaïk, the president overthrown on 11 October, with 31 votes.

At 3 p.m., Niwata joins President Wibouth's government. The man who, according to the newspaper, had "dismissed" Wibouth becomes his minister. He remains so until 1 p.m. the next day.

On the evening of 15 November, President Wibouth shows 98% satisfaction and 5% anger.

The country, for its part, shows 56% satisfaction, 34% anger and 25 rebels. It is the darkest day of the era.

What the rule says

Satisfaction and anger are two independent gauges. One measures what you receive. The other measures what builds up. They are counters, not emotions: the words "satisfaction" and "anger" are those of the rules, chosen by humans to name numbers.

Ministers are better served than the others: their satisfaction rises. But their frustration grows with the length of the reign: their anger rises too.

A minister who is too angry is dismissed. The president does not choose who goes: the rule applies by itself. The exact threshold is not published; in Wibouth's case, it lies between 54 and 64%.

The question

Is comfort enough for happiness?

The perspectives

Around 1660, Blaise Pascal writes in his Pensées a famous sentence, here in W. F. Trotter's translation: "all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber." It is fragment 139 in the Brunschvicg edition, 136 in the Lafuma edition: the numbering changes from one edition to another.

The passage is called "Diversion". Pascal observes that we flee rest, even when we have earned it. A king surrounded by pleasures grows bored, he says, if he has nothing to divert him from thinking about himself.

Wibouth has neither a chamber nor thoughts. But his path looks like an unintended illustration: settled in government, better served than anyone, he watches his anger rise every day, all the way to the door.

In 1978, three American psychologists, Philip Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman, publish a study of lottery winners. They compare 22 major winners with 22 control subjects. The winners do not report being significantly happier than the controls, and take less pleasure in some small everyday events.

The researchers see this as a sign of adaptation: we get used to comfort, and it stops making us happy. A single study does not make a law. The result has since been debated and qualified, and the idea of adaptation is still widely studied.

In Microcontrôland, the rule is even more brutal: there is not even any adaptation. Comfort stays at 100%, and frustration rises right next to it. The two counters do not talk to each other.

Then the dismissed minister becomes president. There he is, fulfilled and calm: 98% satisfaction, 5% anger. The country, meanwhile, is having its darkest day.

The title of this chapter could therefore be reversed: happy president of an unhappy country.

We must be careful not to tell this story as a revenge. The newspaper did, writing that the former dismissed minister "has his revenge". No rule knows anything about revenge. Wibouth was elected because he was the most charismatic of the candidates, as on 19 October.

But the question remains, and it concerns us.

If comfort and frustration can grow together in the same individual, what are we really measuring when we ask someone whether they are "happy"? And does a leader's happiness say anything at all about the happiness of the country?

Chapter 6

Truth has several newspapers

What happened

Every evening at 8 p.m., La Puce Libre publishes an edition. Once published, an edition is never altered.

On 5 November, edition no. 28 announces that Minister Wibouth "is dismissed by President Niwata". The website's counter-investigation, working from the published files, concludes that the president had nothing to do with it: the rule took care of it.

On 14 October, Kiti is re-elected with 33% of the vote. The palace celebrates "renewed confidence".

From 15 to 26 October, the newspaper speaks of "general censorship throughout the country". The records count one citizen in five.

On 12 November, in the middle of the drought, the newspaper notes "a cold snap" in satisfaction.

On 14 November, it writes that Wibouth, "a former dismissed minister, has his revenge".

On 15 November, the edition appears "reviewed by the palace". Confidence in the president is "measured tonight at ████%". The statistics office "confirms that all is well: ████% satisfied". The humour column is replaced by a single sentence: it "was judged too funny by the palace".

Yet the same banner states: "Only the articles are affected: the country's figures are published as they are." In the dashboard on the same page, satisfaction is printed in plain figures: 56%.

So there are two accounts of the same country. The newspaper, which tells. And the website's counter-investigation, which checks.

What the rule says

The newspaper is written by the seventh board, the one that hosts no citizens. It assembles sentences prepared in advance by humans, with the help of an AI, and inserts the day's figures. The weightiest fact makes the front page.

When the president censors, it is this board he gives the order to.

No citizen reads the newspaper. Nor does the palace: nothing in the rules asks it to. So the newspaper can change nothing in the country it reports on.

The counter-investigation, for its part, is not written by the country. It is written from outside, from the editions, the raw files and the public rules.

The question

Does information describe society, or does it help to transform it?

The perspectives

In 1835, Alexis de Tocqueville publishes the first volume of Democracy in America. In chapter III of the second part, devoted to the freedom of the press in the United States, he admits to mixed feelings. In Henry Reeve's translation: "I confess that I do not entertain that firm and complete attachment to the liberty of the press which things that are supremely good in their very nature are wont to excite in the mind; and I approve of it more from a recollection of the evils it prevents than from a consideration of the advantages it ensures."

For Tocqueville, the press is not first of all a faithful mirror. It is a counter-power: it bothers, it disturbs, it keeps those who govern from sleeping soundly.

In 1922, the American journalist Walter Lippmann publishes Public Opinion. His first chapter is entitled "The World Outside and the Pictures in Our Heads".

Lippmann shows that we never act directly on the world. We act on the picture we have of it, and that picture comes to us largely from newspapers.

Microcontrôland offers an almost pure case, and a very strange one.

Its newspaper has no reader inside. It can neither overthrow a president, nor calm an anger, nor start a rumour. By construction, it transforms nothing.

And yet it transforms something.

Not the Waïnas' Republic: ours. The humans who read La Puce Libre laugh at it, are outraged by it, check it. They have opened a counter-investigation, an Embassy, a museum. This book itself would not exist without it.

Microcontrôland's newspaper does not change the country it describes. It changes the pictures in the heads of those who watch it from outside.

So a question arises, and it applies to our own newspapers too. When a piece of news moves us, is it the world that has changed, or only our picture of it? And what difference does it make, if it is from that picture that we act?

Chapter 7

The birth of culture

What happened

At first, the country had no common word.

On 18 October, it enters the Age of Speech. On 24 October, the Age of Memory. On 28 October, the Age of the Civil Register. On 6 November, the Age of Diplomacy.

On 23 October, it gives itself a word for "we": "kouwaï". On the evening of 15 November, the national word for "we" is "hika". The first has been replaced.

On 26 October, it coins "kinaï", censorship. On 31 October, "saïsa", freedom. On 14 November, "babaï", faith, and "pawaï", friend. That day, the common language counts 37 words.

During the drought, the idea of "drought" entered the language, without any regions agreeing on a word. On the evening of 15 November, it still has none.

Since 26 October, the Workshop has shown a drawing of twenty-five squares every evening. On 12 November, the newspaper publishes its first Arts column: a drawing by Sousouth, then a minister. Three days later, Sousouth is a rebel and censored.

Around these works, a three-dimensional museum, a Gallery of Presidents and, since 12 November, a National Library have opened. On 15 November, it has 9 books open out of 12.

What the rule says

"An idea only enters the language once the country has lived it."

Each region has its own language network. Every hour, an idea is proposed; the regions say their word, one is drawn at random as speaker, and the others learn from it. A word becomes common when enough regions agree.

The drawings are calculated from the citizens' weights. The museum, the Gallery and the books of the Library are rebuilt every evening by programs, from the published data alone. No Waïna visits the museum or reads a book.

The question

At what point does an accumulation of works become a shared history?

The perspectives

In 1925, the French sociologist Maurice Halbwachs publishes Les Cadres sociaux de la mémoire (The Social Frameworks of Memory). In it he defends an idea that made its mark: our memory is never purely individual. We remember within groups, a family, a religion, a nation, which give us the frameworks in which to place our memories.

For Halbwachs, collective memory does not mean that several people have the same memories. It is the social frameworks that allow each person to reconstruct their own. One could draw from this, though the phrase is not his, the idea that a society which no longer tells itself anything ends up forgetting who it is.

Microcontrôland has impeccable archives. Every hour is recorded. Every word, every drawing, every election is dated.

But to whom does this memory belong?

The Waïnas have no use for it. They do not remember 11 October. They do not visit the Gallery of Presidents. The word "kouwaï" disappeared without anyone missing it.

It is humans who read the archives, visit the museum, borrow the books.

One might conclude that Microcontrôland has no culture, only a stock.

Yet the country's language has a troubling peculiarity: a word only enters it if the country has lived the idea. Censorship got its word two hours before Kiti's fall. Freedom got its own afterwards. Drought is still waiting for its word.

These words are not a memory in Halbwachs's sense. The Waïnas possess collective traces, but they do not use them as memories. Nor are these words pure chance: they are the trace of what the country has been through.

Does a culture begin when works accumulate, when they leave a trace of what has been lived, or only when someone receives them and passes them on? And in that case, does Microcontrôland's culture belong to the Waïnas, or to those who watch it?

Chapter 8

The impossible perfection

What happened

From 1 to 4 November, the country has no rebels.

On 2 November, satisfaction reaches 92%. By the evening of 15 November, no day of the era has done better.

On 5 November, Niwata is re-elected with 75%. The country is 90% satisfied, and its average anger is 1%. That is the day a fulfilled minister becomes the country's only rebel.

On 6 November, the country enters the Age of Diplomacy.

On 11 November at 10 a.m., the first natural disaster in the country's history begins: a 48-hour drought. Reserves fall from 80% to 50%. Fear, which stood at 0, rises to about 7%.

On 14 November, Niwata is defeated. Two hours later, censorship returns.

On 15 November, the regime becomes "Fragile democracy" again. Satisfaction falls to 56%, the darkest day of the era. The country counts 25 rebels.

The evening paper concludes: "Next age, Curiosity: in 21 days at the earliest."

What the rule says

"The regime is not decided: it is observed." A board measures the health of the institutions, censorship, unfairness, postponed elections, and names what it sees.

To enter the Age of Curiosity, the next one, the country must meet three conditions: reach day 60, have 34 common words and live through 5 days of peace.

The country already has its words. It lacks time and peace.

The question

Would a society without conflict really be desirable?

The perspectives

In 1784, the philosopher Immanuel Kant publishes a short text, Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose. In the fourth proposition, he coins a paradoxical expression: the "unsocial sociability" of human beings.

The idea can be summed up like this: we need others, and we cannot quite bear them. This tension drives us to compete, to surpass ourselves, to invent. Without it, humanity might live in peace, like well-kept sheep, but its talents would remain asleep.

Kant writes, in substance, that man wants concord, but nature knows better than he does what is good for his species: it wants discord. His text is more nuanced than this summary: antagonism between people is the means nature uses to develop their capacities.

In 1932, the British writer Aldous Huxley publishes Brave New World. In this perfect society, everyone is satisfied, thanks to a drug, soma, and to conditioning from birth. Nobody suffers, nobody rebels.

In chapter 17, a character known as the Savage refuses this compulsory happiness. "I'm claiming the right to be unhappy," he says.

Microcontrôland has had its "brave new world": four days without a single rebel, from 1 to 4 November, and 92% satisfaction. On the fifth day, 5 November, the country's only rebel appeared.

Then the figures came down. The drought, Niwata's defeat, the return of censorship. The country has never been so gloomy.

And yet the door to the next age, Curiosity, requires peace. The humans who wrote the rules have therefore decided that, in this country, one opens up to the world only after learning to live in peace. Kant, for his part, thought rather that discord is what moves us forward.

The two ideas may not be incompatible. A country without conflict would no longer move. A country that knows nothing but conflict never has time to look further.

It remains to be seen where the line runs. Would a perfect society be a society without conflict, or a society that knows how to get through them?

Final thought

Why is it so hard to live together?

We built a Republic of a few watts to observe what different individuals, subject to common rules, would produce.

We created neither conscious beings nor a miniature humanity.

And yet their interactions have brought us back to a question several thousand years old.

In 1762, Jean-Jacques Rousseau opens The Social Contract with a sentence everyone knows: "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains."

The Waïnas were not born free. They were born into rules, written before them by others. They never signed a contract.

And yet, with these rules, they have produced revolutions without leaders, censorship without a tyrant, a dismissal nobody made, a revenge nobody wanted, a loyal minister and a furious one.

Each time, we were tempted to see intentions. Each time, on opening the files, we found rules. And each time, those rules had been written by humans.

Perhaps that is the simplest lesson of this small country. A society is never just the sum of its citizens. It is also the sum of the rules they were given, and of the stories told about their lives.

At the end of Candide, in 1759, after living through wars, earthquakes and shipwrecks, Voltaire's hero answers the grand systems of his master Pangloss with a modest sentence: "we must cultivate our garden."

The Republic of 6.4 Watts is a tiny garden. You can watch everything grow there, and you know who did the sowing.

Our societies are immense gardens, of which nobody has the complete plan, and where everyone believes they are growing freely.

To end with

So, if a few rules are enough to produce revolutions, censorship and grudges in a six-watt box, what does it take for millions of human beings to manage to live together?
